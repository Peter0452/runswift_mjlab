Learned striker by self-play — plan#

Living plan for training a K1 striker with self-play / multi-agent RL and plugging it into the runswift behaviour. Branch: claude/inspiring-thompson-i5igqs.

1. Goal#

Demonstrate a learned striker: continuous ball handling, dribbling, kicking and outmanoeuvring the opposing striker to score, within the system’s constraints (deploy joint / torque limits, the robot’s real perception, the runswift behaviour’s rules and roles).

Everything else is abstracted and given: referee states, roles, restarts, localisation, getting up, teammates. The striker runs only when the behaviour says open play, you are the striker.

2. Problem as trained#

  • Game: 1v1, striker against striker (runswift’s RoleCoordinator makes the robot closest to the ball each team’s striker, so the robot our striker meets at the ball is the other team’s striker).

  • Field: per episode 9 x 6 m up to 14 x 9 m (width scales with length), random heading, placed around the spawned ball and robot. Goals 2.4 m wide, crossbar 1.6 m (small) / 1.8 m (medium, length >= 11.5 m).

  • Goal: whole ball over the goal line between the posts, under the bar. Over any other line: out. Either ends the point; a kickoff follows on the same field (ball ahead of the learner, opponent at the learner’s spot mirrored through the ball). Episodes end on falls or time out (30 s).

  • Opponent: frozen copies of the learner (mirror) or members of a pool of past selves and seeds. It is not trained on its own.

Two-level striker (controller over skills)#

Controller every 0.2 s (10 env steps), 4 outputs in [−1, 1]: a0 kick (> 0) or walk, a1:3 direction (cos, sin) in the robot frame, a3 kick range (thirds) or walk speed (0–1.2 m/s). Inputs: the 86 above + current skill + time since switch (88). Skills (frozen):

  • Kick: stage-3 kick loop (h022_c2_x50); aim / range slots overwritten with the controller’s choice.

  • Walk: AMP walk model_9950 (wwcmu50 stageA); velocity command from direction and speed (turns towards the direction). Dribble = walk through the ball.

Both skills drive the same 22 joints, so switching only changes which network runs.

6. Training recipe (option 2: alternate controller and skills)#

Phase 1  controller learns, skills frozen       Mjlab-Velocity-Striker-Controller-Booster-K1
Phase 2  kick skill learns inside the game       Mjlab-Velocity-Kick-SelfPlay-Amp-DA-Muon-Booster-K1
Phase 3  controller re-learns with the new kick  phase-1 task, new kick checkpoint
... repeat; optional final: distil into one flat policy and self-play it end-to-end

# Phase 1
uv run train Mjlab-Velocity-Striker-Controller-Booster-K1 --env.scene.num-envs 4096
# Phase 2: kick fine-tunes against the phase-1 striker; aims are lanes across
# the goal mouth (up to 40 % of its width) so the kick obeys commanded aims.
uv run train Mjlab-Velocity-Kick-SelfPlay-Amp-DA-Muon-Booster-K1 \
  --env.actions.opponent.controller-checkpoint <phase1>/model_N.pt
# Phase 3
uv run train Mjlab-Velocity-Striker-Controller-Booster-K1 \
  --env.actions.opponent.kick-skill-checkpoint <phase2>/model_M.pt

Guardrails each round: deploy-safety terms stay on; gate on the runswift kick grid and the 90-case score grid (runswift_code/behaviour/examples/booster_match/score-benchmark.md) before accepting a skill round.

7. Integration with the runswift behaviour#

MatchRunner (unchanged gates: playing, striker, no active restart, upright, localised)
  _play_ball  →  learned striker branch
     target = select_target(field, ball, scene, tactics)      # unchanged lane choice
     d = target − ball_field;  aim = rotate(d, −θ)/|d|;  range = bucket(|d|)
     StrikerIntent{active, aim, range, speed caps, stamp, deadline}
policy_runner (50 Hz) StrikerPolicy
  vision → ball (72–74), opponent (83–85);  IMU / joints → body (0–71)
  aim held between intents, turned by IMU yaw change since its stamp
  lease expired / whistle / role change / fall / lost localisation → hand back

Ownership while the striker runs: behaviour decides whether and where to attack; the policy decides how; the runner’s head tracker owns the head; the existing get-up routine handles falls.

No new odometry or localisation: the ball memory uses IMU yaw only (as trained), the opponent is single-frame detections, and the aim uses the behaviour’s existing field pose. Standalone (no behaviour): aim from a fixed IMU heading (--kick-dir-mode field) or through the ball, operator-supervised.

9. Next (in order)#

  1. Ball-out restart fix: after an out, give the kickoff to the opponent (ball ahead of it), optionally a small penalty. Today an out hands the learner a fresh ball in front of it — a loophole.

  2. Aim realism: noise on slots 78–80 (per-episode heading bias, jitter, occasional jumps) for localisation error; occasional aim-lane switches mid-point (as select_target does when a lane closes).

  3. Opponent sampling (OpenAI lessons): longer snapshot history (e.g. 32 every 100 iterations, uniform over the recent half), mirror share ~0.2.

  4. Anneal task shaping (OpenAI lessons): kick shaping towards ~20 % over the first 3–5k iterations; deploy-safety terms fixed.

  5. Pushing constraint: cap robot–robot contact force (c.<term> adaptive constraints), so play is legal standalone.

  6. First GPU runs: phase 1, then phase 2; fixed-opponent evaluations (h022, walker, runswift scripted striker) logged alongside self-play win rates.

  7. Opponent motion: frame-stacked detections or a GRU controller.

  8. Integration: StrikerIntent in the behaviour, StrikerPolicy in the runner, SDK ↔ low-level mode handoff timing on the K1.

  9. Later: scripted runswift goalie (blocking_target) on the pitch; physical goal posts; learned defender / 2v1.

11. Risks#

  • Non-stationary opponents: the critic does not know which opponent it faces.

  • Shooting earlier instead of outmanoeuvring (stage-3 kick shaping still pays).

  • Phase 2 trains the kick on its own decisions plus random aims, not inside the controller’s decisions (an approximation).

  • Localisation may degrade while the head tracks the ball during long plays.

  • Controller-mode switching on the real K1 (SDK walking / soccer vs low-level) is unmeasured.

12. References#

  • Bansal et al., Emergent Complexity via Multi-Agent Competition, ICLR 2018 (arXiv 1710.03748); code openai/multiagent-competition — PPO, dense-shaping curriculum annealed to win / lose, opponents sampled from past snapshots.

  • Su et al., Toward Real-World Cooperative and Competitive Soccer with Quadrupedal Robot Teams, CoRL 2025 (arXiv 2505.13834) — frozen skills + learned high-level policy (MAPPO, fictitious self-play), real Go1 robots.

  • runswift behaviour: runswift_code/behaviour/scripts/match_runner.py, match_tactics.py.
